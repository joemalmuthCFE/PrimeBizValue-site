What's actually in the report

You can get a valuation number free in about ninety seconds — from us, from twenty broker calculators, or from an AI chatbot. This page is about the other thing: the document you hand to a franchisor, a lender, a partner, or the person across the table from you. Here is every section of it, worked end to end on a real-shaped set of numbers.

Everything below is an illustrative example. “Harborline Mechanical” is a business we made up for this page. It is not a customer, and none of these figures came from a real report. We have not published any customer's numbers and we won't. The structure, the calculations, and the data sources are exactly what a real report uses.

The example business

1. The recast worksheet

This is the section a free calculator structurally cannot produce, because a calculator never sees your general ledger. Recasting restates the P&L as it would look to a buyer: earnings before one full-time working owner's compensation, before financing, before non-cash charges, and before anything that will not recur under new ownership. The result is Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE).

Two things make this worksheet worth paying for. It is itemized — every adjustment is a separate line with a dollar amount, not a lump “add-backs: $317,800”. And every line carries a documentation status, because an add-back you cannot evidence will be struck out by the buyer's accountant, and you should find that out now rather than in diligence.

What the second reader sees. A buyer's accountant, a franchisor's transfer desk, and a credit analyst all do the same thing with a valuation: they look for the add-backs and try to remove them. A worksheet that has already flagged its own weak lines survives that review. A single “add-backs” total does not.

2. Where the multiple comes from

The most common complaint about automated valuations is that a multiple appears with no explanation. This section shows the derivation as arithmetic you can check.

Report section 2 of 5 · Multiple derivation

Step one — the size band. On Main Street the strongest determinant of the multiple is the size of the cash flow, not the industry label. Defensible SDE of $401,000 places this business in the under-$500K band.

Cash flow bandAverage multiple paid
Under $500K SDE — this business2.0×
$500K – $1M SDE2.5×
$1M – $2M SDE2.8×

Source: IBBA / M&A Source Market Pulse, Q1 2026.

Why not 2.61×? Across all reported 2025 transactions the average sale-price-to-cash-flow multiple was 2.61× (BizBuySell 2025 Year in Review) — and you will see that number quoted at you. It is a blended average across every deal size in the market. Applying it to a sub-$500K-SDE business overstates the value by roughly 30%. The report states which comparator it used and why, so a lender or a buyer can agree or disagree with the choice rather than with a black box.

Step two — the adjustments. Each one moves the multiple within the band, and each is tied to something observable in your submission:

Adjustment weights are the model's, not a published standard. The report prints them so you can argue with them. That is the point — a number you can argue with is a number you can defend.

3. The valuation range

A single number is a sales tactic. A range with a stated basis is a negotiating position.

Indicated range: $800,000 – $960,000, on a debt-free, cash-free basis. Transferable parts and materials inventory of $38,000 at cost is conveyed in addition, as is normal in an asset sale.

Context for the range, not a prediction: the median reported Main Street sale price in 2025 was $350,000 on median cash flow of $158,950, and businesses that sold achieved 94% of asking price over 169–207 days on market (BizBuySell 2025). This business is materially above the market median on cash flow, which is why it prices above the median sale price.

The “0.75× revenue” number the owner was given was $1,390,000. The report does not simply say that is wrong. It shows what would have to be true for it to be right: defensible SDE of roughly $560,000 to $630,000, depending on which size band the business lands in, against $401,000 today. That is a conversation the owner can have with a buyer. “My friend said 0.75× revenue” is not.

4. The bankability screen

Most Main Street businesses are bought with borrowed money. So the real question underneath the valuation is not “what is it worth” — it is “at this price, does the deal service its own debt?” If it doesn't, the buyer pool collapses to cash buyers, and cash buyers pay less. This section runs that arithmetic before anyone spends money finding out.

What the report concludes here. Coverage holds across the entire indicated range, and does not fall to a 1.25× threshold until roughly a $1.68M price. So financing is not the binding constraint on this deal — buyer willingness to pay is. That is a genuinely useful answer, and it is the opposite of what a seller is usually told.

Not every business gets that answer. The screen bites hardest when SDE is under about $250,000, when the incoming owner needs a large salary, or when the amortization is short. Those are exactly the deals that die three weeks into underwriting. 1.25× is a coverage level commonly applied in acquisition underwriting; your lender sets its own threshold and you should ask what it is.

Read this before you put it in a loan file. This is a pre-application screen, not an SBA-compliant business valuation, and we will never describe it as one. Under SBA SOP 50 10 8, when the financed intangible portion of a deal exceeds $250,000 the independent valuation must be ordered by the lender from an appraiser holding an ASA, CBA, ABV, CVA, AVA, or BCA credential and independent of loan production. You cannot buy that valuation yourself, from us or from anyone. What you can do is find out, before you spend three weeks and someone else's underwriting fee, whether the deal you are contemplating is likely to clear coverage at all. That is what this section is for.

We are not tax advisers and this is not tax advice. The report uses the effective rate you supply and shows the arithmetic; your CPA supplies the rate. The reason it is in the document at all is that this is the number that decides whether you can actually retire on the deal — and it is the number no free calculator will ever show you.

What this document is not

We would rather lose a $199 sale than have a report bounce out of a loan file or a courtroom. So, plainly:

  • It is not a certified or accredited appraisal. Our “Prime Valuation Certification” is our own internal standard. It is not an accreditation from any professional body, and we say so on the certification page rather than burying it.
  • It is not a USPAP-compliant valuation report and it is not signed by a credentialed appraiser.
  • It is not an SBA-compliant independent business valuation. See the box above — the lender orders that one, not you.
  • It is not admissible for divorce, probate, estate tax, or litigation. Courts and the IRS require credentialed work (ASA, CVA, ABV). If that is your situation, do not buy this — email us and we will point you to the right kind of professional. We would rather refer you out than take your money for something that will be thrown out.
  • It is not an audit. We do not verify your figures, visit your premises, interview your management, call your landlord, or read your lease. The report is only as good as what you put into it, and it says so on its own first page.
  • It is not a guarantee of sale price. Nobody can give you that. What sells a business is a buyer.
  • It is not a listing agreement. We are not brokers. We do not take a commission, we do not want your listing, and we have no financial interest in your number being high.
  • It does not yet model franchise-specific mechanics as a separate engine. Transfer fees, ROFR windows, remaining agreement term, and territory rights are captured as narrative risk and upside factors today, not as a dedicated franchise model. If you are transferring a franchised unit, that distinction matters and you should know it before you buy.
