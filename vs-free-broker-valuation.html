A free broker valuation, and what it is actually for

A business broker will value your business for nothing. That is not a trick and we are not going to imply otherwise. It is a real service, honestly offered, and for a lot of sellers it is the right thing to use. This page is about the one question it structurally cannot answer, and how to tell whether that question applies to you.

First, the correction we made to our own site

We used to compare our $199 report to broker valuations “costing $2,500–$10,000.” That was wrong and we took it down. That range is accurate for formal credentialed appraisals and only for those. When an owner phones a broker and asks what their business is worth, the price they are quoted is almost always zero. Anybody who has actually made that call knows it, and a comparison that fails on the first phone call poisons everything else on the page.

How the free valuation is paid for

Not by you, at the time. It is bundled into the commission the brokerage earns when your business sells — commonly around 10% on Main Street deals. Brokerages state this openly, and industry guidance says plainly that a broker's opinion of value carries no separate charge. It is a marketing cost that converts into a listing.

The same logic drives the free calculators. One Florida broker, reviewing free valuation calculators including his own, said the quiet part out loud twice over: none of them are accurate in the appraisal sense, they are starting ranges — and he built his hoping you would want the detailed follow-up. He also listed exactly what no calculator can do: verify your add-backs, judge owner dependency, weigh customer concentration, or read your lease. We agree with all of it, including about our own free estimate.

What a broker gives you that we cannot

This is not a courtesy paragraph. These are real advantages and you should weigh them.

  • Local, recent, private transaction knowledge. A good broker knows what actually closed in your county in the last eighteen months, at what terms, and to whom. That is not in any published dataset we or anyone else can buy.
  • Marketability, not just value. Whether your business will attract five buyers or none is a different question from what it is worth, and it is the one that determines what you actually get.
  • A buyer list. A valuation does not sell a business. A broker with active buyers might.
  • Judgement on presentation. Which weaknesses to fix before listing, and which to disclose early rather than have discovered in diligence.
  • They run the process. Confidentiality, buyer screening, offer management, and the long grind to close.

If you intend to list with a broker, get the free valuation. Get two or three. We are not trying to talk you out of it, and we would rather say so here than have you discover we were selling against something free and useful.

The one question it structurally cannot answer

A broker's opinion of value is produced by a person whose income depends on winning your listing and on a deal closing. That is not an accusation of bad faith — most brokers are straight, and an overpriced listing is a broker's own worst outcome, because they carry it for months and earn nothing. It is an observation about incentives, and incentives are structural.

Here is the consequence that matters. No sell-side business can publish the sentence “the deal in front of you is overpriced — walk away.” A brokerage that said it would stop getting listings. So while free valuations are everywhere, there is no free valuation in this market written for the buyer. If you are buying — a business, a franchised unit, a partner's share — the only number you have been shown was produced for the other side of the table. That gap is not a marketing position. Questions people actually ask

Is a business broker's valuation really free?

Usually, yes. A broker's opinion of value is generally not sold as a product; it is bundled into the commission earned if you list with them and the business sells. Brokerages say so openly, and industry guidance is explicit that a broker's opinion of value carries no separate charge. Anyone comparing their own paid product to a broker valuation 'costing thousands' is comparing you to a price you will not be quoted.

Should I get a free valuation from a broker?

If you are a seller who intends to list, yes - and get more than one. A good broker brings something we genuinely cannot: knowledge of what actually closed in your market recently, who the active buyers are, and how marketable your particular business will be. That is worth having, and it costs you nothing but a phone call.

So why would I pay $199 for a valuation instead?

Three reasons, and none of them is accuracy. First, whose interest the number serves: a broker's opinion is produced by someone whose income depends on winning your listing and on the deal closing. Second, portability: a free opinion of value is rarely an itemized document you can hand to a franchisor, a lender, or a buyer's accountant. Third, no sales call: every free tool we have looked at asks for a phone number and routes you to an advisor. If you are the buyer rather than the seller, there is a fourth reason - no free valuation in this market is written for you.

Are broker valuations inflated?

We are not going to claim that, because we cannot prove it and plenty of brokers price realistically - a broker who overprices a listing carries it for months and earns nothing. The honest version is narrower and harder to argue with: the number is produced by someone with a financial interest in the outcome, and you are not shown the workings. You can hold both thoughts at once.

Can I use a broker valuation and a PrimeBizValue report together?

That is the sensible thing to do, and it is what we would do. Take the broker's opinion for market knowledge and marketability. Use the report for the documented recast, the derivation of the multiple, and the net-cash-at-close arithmetic. Where they disagree, the gap is the conversation worth having - and now you have the workings to have it with.
