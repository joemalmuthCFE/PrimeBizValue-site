You are about to sign a document that takes you off the market for 30 to 90 days, and the only valuation you have been shown was produced by the person selling the business. Ten things to check before you sign an LOI

This is free, it stands alone, and you do not need us to use it. Work through it with the seller's package in front of you. If you cannot answer one of these, that is the diligence item.

  1. 1Which clauses are actually binding. The price is usually non-binding. The exclusivity or no-shop, the confidentiality provision, the expense allocation, and the governing law usually are. You are trading a binding promise for a non-binding number, so know exactly what you have promised and for how long. Have a lawyer read it — this is the cheapest legal hour in the whole deal.
  2. 2How long exclusivity runs, and what happens when diligence slips. Sixty days sounds generous until the seller's accountant takes three weeks to produce a general ledger. Ask for an extension mechanism tied to information delivery, not to the calendar.
  3. 3What earnings figure the price is built on. SDE and EBITDA are not interchangeable, and the difference is a full owner's salary. A 3x multiple on SDE and a 3x multiple on EBITDA are wildly different prices for the same business. Make the LOI say which one, and for which period — trailing twelve months, last fiscal year, or an annualised stub.
  4. 4Every add-back, line by line, and whether it can be evidenced. This is where Main Street deals are actually won and lost. Ask for the adjustments as an itemized schedule, not a single “add-backs” total. Then sort them: documented (payroll registers, Form 4562, settlement agreements, carrier invoices) versus asserted (a personal vehicle with no mileage log, sponsorships booked to advertising, “one-time” costs that appear in all three years). Every dollar of undocumented add-back is a dollar of earnings times the multiple in purchase price.
  5. 5What happens to the business when the owner leaves. Who holds the licence, the key customer relationships, the pricing authority, the technical knowledge? If the owner sells, estimates, and closes, you are buying a job with a payroll attached. Ask what the second-in-command earns and whether they know the business is for sale.
  6. 6Customer concentration, and contract transferability. Get revenue by customer for three years. Then check whether the contracts survive a change of control — assignment clauses, consent requirements, and termination-on-sale provisions can silently convert a 40% customer into a 0% customer at closing.
  7. 7The lease. Remaining term, renewal options, the rent under those options, assignment rights, whether the landlord's consent can be withheld, and whether the landlord is related to the seller. A business with two years of remaining term is a two-year business. Read it before the LOI, not after.
  8. 8Exactly what you are buying. Asset or stock. Debt-free and cash-free, or not. Whether inventory is included or conveyed at cost on top. What working capital comes with the business, and how it is measured and trued up at close. Which vehicles and equipment are in and which the owner is keeping. Vagueness here is not neutral: every ambiguity gets resolved at closing, under time pressure, by whoever is less willing to walk.
  9. 9Whether the deal services its own debt at that price. Take the earnings, subtract a real market salary for whoever runs the business, subtract a capital replacement reserve, and compare what is left to the annual debt service on the loan you would actually need. If coverage is thin, the price is not a negotiating preference — it is a financing constraint, and you will discover it in underwriting at your own expense. The arithmetic, worked through, is here.
  10. 10The seller note, the transition, and the non-compete. A seller who will carry paper is a seller who believes their own numbers; a seller who insists on all cash at close is telling you something. Pin down the transition period in weeks and hours, in writing. And check the non-compete's scope, radius, and duration — and whether it binds the seller's spouse and children, who in a family business are the real competitive risk.
One more, free of charge. Ask the seller directly: “What would you fix first if you were keeping it another five years?” The answer is your capital expenditure plan, and it is rarely in the package.

Where a valuation actually helps you

Not as ammunition. As a second reading of the same financials, produced by a method you can show the seller.

  • It re-cuts the seller's earnings figure. Same P&L, itemized adjustments, a documentation status on each one. You find out which add-backs will not survive before you have spent money on diligence.
  • It gives you a benchmarked multiple rather than an asserted one. Size band, published transaction data, and every adjustment that moved the number, printed as arithmetic. When you negotiate, you are not arguing feelings; you are showing a derivation the seller can check.
  • It tells you what the deal does under debt. The Deal Report runs a coverage screen at your price and structure, before you spend anything on an application.
  • It works across several targets. Most buyers look at multiple businesses before closing one. A consistent method applied to each is how you compare them honestly.

And the plain limitation: we work from what you give us. We do not verify the seller's figures, visit the premises, or read the lease. If the seller's P&L is fiction, so is anything built on it — which is exactly why item 4 above is item 4.

Questions people actually ask

Is a letter of intent binding?

Usually partly. The commercial terms - price, structure, allocation - are normally expressed as non-binding, while exclusivity or no-shop, confidentiality, governing law, and who pays expenses are normally binding and enforceable. That mix is the point: you are giving the seller a binding promise not to look elsewhere in exchange for a non-binding price. Read which clauses are carved out as binding before you sign, and have a lawyer do the same.

Why is every free business valuation written for the seller?

Because of who pays for it. Broker opinions of value are bundled into a sale commission, marketplace valuations are funded by listings, and bank and insurer calculators are funded by loans and policies. Each is a real service, honestly offered, and each is paid for by someone who benefits when a business is listed, sold, or financed. We have not found a free valuation product in the Main Street market that is built for the buyer, and there is a structural reason for that: a sell-side business cannot credibly publish the sentence 'this deal is overpriced.'

How much should I pay for a small business?

There is no universal answer, but there is a reference frame. In 2025 the median reported Main Street sale price was $350,000 on median cash flow of $158,950, and the average sale-price-to-cash-flow multiple across all reported transactions was 2.61x - a blended figure across every deal size. Size band is a better guide than the blended average: average multiples of roughly 2.0x under $500K of SDE, 2.5x from $500K to $1M, and 2.8x from $1M to $2M. Adjust from there for owner dependency, customer concentration, recurring revenue, trend, and the lease.

What should I check before signing an LOI?

At minimum: which clauses are binding, how long exclusivity runs, what earnings figure the price is built on, every add-back and whether it can be evidenced, owner dependency, customer concentration, the lease term and assignment rights, exactly what assets and working capital are included, whether the deal services its own debt at that price, and what the seller note, transition, and non-compete look like. The ten-point checklist on this page walks through each one.

Can I get a valuation on a business I do not own yet?

Yes. You need the seller's profit and loss statement, which is normally provided under NDA before an LOI is signed. If the seller will not release financials before you sign exclusivity, that itself is information about the process you are entering.
