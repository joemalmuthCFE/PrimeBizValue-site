Selling your franchise: you are not just finding a buyer, you are asking permission

A franchised unit does not change hands the way an independent business does. There is a third party at the table who did not sign your listing agreement, has no deadline, and can say no. Here is the mechanical reality of a transfer, what actually moves the resale price, and — honestly — which parts of it our report handles today and which parts you have to handle yourself.

The one-paragraph version

Roughly 4.1% of US franchise units transferred in 2024, a five-year high — on the order of 34,000 units a year changing hands. Every one of those transfers required a franchisor's written approval, most sat through a right-of-first-refusal window, and nearly all carried a transfer fee running from about $5,000 to $50,000 or more per unit. Franchisees go into that process with a price they got from a broker who wants the listing, from another franchisee at a convention, or from nowhere at all. The number is the cheap part. A number with its basis written down is what survives a transfer desk.

Transfer rate: FRANdata, 2024. The ~34,000 unit figure is our own arithmetic from that rate applied to the US franchise unit base — treat it as an order of magnitude, not a census.

The structural problem nobody else will name for you. Your franchisor holds more data about what units in your system earn and what they sell for than any broker, any calculator, and any appraiser. Your franchisor is also the party least able to tell you. Unit-level financial figures handed to a franchisee start to look like a financial performance representation, which is the territory of Item 19 of the FDD and the FTC Franchise Rule — so most systems simply decline. The best-informed party in your transaction is structurally silent. That is why franchisees end up pricing units off convention hallway talk.

Two consequences worth internalising. First, the ROFR is not a formality — it means your negotiated price becomes an option the franchisor can exercise, so a deliberately inflated ask has a real cost and a deliberately cheap deal can simply be taken. Second, a nine-to-fifteen-month clock means the financials you price on today are not the financials the buyer will close on. Both argue for a documented basis you can re-run rather than a single number in an email.

What moves a franchise resale price that a generic calculator never sees

A standard small-business valuation asks about earnings, owner dependency, customer concentration, and the lease. All of that still applies. These are the factors on top of it, and they are specific to owning a franchised unit:

No Item 19 in the FDDAbsent in 35–40% of brands; associated with 10–15% discountsWithout published system financial performance data, a buyer and a buyer's lender have nothing to benchmark your P&L against, so both price in uncertainty.

Ranges as documented across franchise resale practice. They are directional guidance, not a formula, and they vary by brand, by market, and by deal.

Be careful with brand multiples you find online. Published EBITDA multiples exist for large systems and get quoted freely at franchisees. They are drawn from multi-unit and institutional transactions, where a portfolio of units with a management layer trades at a materially higher multiple than one owner-operated store. Applying a system-level multiple to a single unit is one of the most expensive mistakes in franchise resale, and it usually runs in the seller's favour right up until the buyer's lender declines.

Your transfer checklist

  1. Pull your franchise agreement and read the transfer clause and the ROFR clause. Note the notice period, the approval standard, the fee, and whether the franchisor can require you to sign a general release.
  2. Confirm the transfer fee in writing from your franchisor, and confirm who pays it.
  3. Count your remaining term and your remaining lease term in months. Establish whether the buyer signs your agreement or the then-current one, and get the current royalty, ad-fund, technology, and remodel obligations in writing.
  4. Ask whether a remodel or equipment refresh is due in the next 24 months. An imminent capital requirement is a price negotiation whether or not anybody raises it.
  5. Talk to your landlord early about assignment. Landlord consent kills more transfers than franchisors do, and it can arrive with a rent increase attached.
  6. Get your add-backs documented before you market the unit. An add-back you cannot evidence is not an add-back; it is a discount you take later, in front of the buyer's accountant.
  7. Ask the franchisor what the transfer desk actually requires in the packet. Some want a valuation; many want a signed purchase agreement and a qualified buyer. Find out rather than assume.
  8. Check whether your buyer's likely financing works at your price before you agree it. If the deal will not service its debt, the approval process is the second thing that kills it.

Questions people actually ask

Can I sell my franchise?

Almost always yes, but not on your own. Essentially every franchise agreement makes a transfer conditional on the franchisor's written approval, and most reserve a right of first refusal that lets the franchisor step into your buyer's shoes on the same terms. You are not selling a business outright; you are assigning an agreement, and the counterparty to that agreement has to consent.

How long does a franchise transfer take?

Franchisor approval commonly runs 90 to 180 days, and a right-of-first-refusal window of 30 to 60 days typically sits inside or ahead of that. From the decision to sell through to closing, 9 to 15 months is a realistic planning assumption for a franchised unit.

What is a franchise transfer fee?

A fee payable to the franchisor for processing and approving the transfer and training the incoming owner. Published amounts commonly run from about $5,000 to $50,000 or more per unit; quick-service restaurant and fitness systems are often in the $25,000 to $50,000 band, and premium systems can exceed $100,000. It is normally the seller's cost unless your agreement or your purchase agreement says otherwise. Read your own franchise agreement rather than relying on any general figure, including this one.

Will my franchisor accept a PrimeBizValue report as the valuation?

We do not know, and anyone who tells you they do is guessing about your system. Franchisors vary widely, and many transfer desks ask for a purchase agreement rather than a valuation. What the report gives you is a documented, dated, itemized basis for the price you are proposing, with the arithmetic printed so a third party can check it. It is not an accredited appraisal and we never describe it as one.

Does PrimeBizValue model franchise transfer fees, ROFR, and territory rights automatically?

Not as a dedicated franchise engine today. The report produces an itemized recast of earnings, a benchmarked multiple with its derivation shown, a valuation range, a net-cash-to-seller waterfall, and, in the Deal Report, a debt-service coverage screen. Franchise-specific mechanics are handled as inputs and as narrative risk and upside factors, not as an automated model. We would rather tell you that before you buy than after.

Why won't my franchisor just tell me what my unit is worth?

Franchisors are generally cautious about giving unit-level financial figures to franchisees, because a number that looks like a projection of financial performance can raise questions under the FTC Franchise Rule's treatment of financial performance representations, which are made in Item 19 of the FDD. The practical result is that the party with the most data about your brand's economics is usually the one party that will not give you a number.
