Yes, ChatGPT can value your business. Here is where that stops working.

Paste your P&L into a good AI model and ask for an SDE recast and a multiple, and you will get a competent answer in thirty seconds for nothing. Pretending otherwise would insult you, and you would catch us at it inside a minute. So let us start with what it does well, and then be specific about the point at which it stops being the right tool.

What an AI model genuinely does well

  • The recast arithmetic. It will find owner compensation, payroll taxes on it, depreciation and amortisation, interest, health insurance, and the obvious personal expenses, and add them back correctly.
  • Explaining the concepts. SDE versus EBITDA, why a buyer cares about owner dependency, what an earn-out does — it explains all of it clearly and patiently, at two in the morning, as many times as you ask.
  • Sanity-checking a number you were given. If a broker or a buyer quoted you something wild, a model will usually tell you it is wild.
  • Drafting the questions to ask. Genuinely useful before a first meeting with a seller or a broker.

If a rough number is all you need this afternoon, use it. Our own free Instant Estimate exists for the same reason: the number is not the scarce thing.

The four places it stops

1. It produces an answer, not an artifact

Almost nobody buys a valuation for themselves. They buy it because somebody else has to be convinced — a franchisor's transfer desk approving a unit transfer, a credit analyst, a buyer's accountant, a business partner, a spouse. That second reader needs a dated document with a stated method, stated inputs, stated limitations, and arithmetic they can follow and disagree with. A chat transcript is not that, no matter how good the reasoning inside it was. This is the whole product, and it is the one thing a chatbot is not trying to be.

2. The multiple comes from recollection, not from a citable dataset

Ask a model for a multiple and you get a number with a confident tone and no provenance: no source, no vintage, no deal-size mix. That matters more than it sounds, because the single biggest error in small-business valuation is applying a blended market average to a business that is nowhere near the average size.

Applying 2.61× to a business with $300,000 of SDE overstates it by roughly 30%. A model will do that cheerfully if that is the figure most represented in what it read.

3. It does not flag which add-backs will die

An AI will add back the personal vehicle. It will not tell you that without a mileage log the buyer's accountant strikes it, and that at a 2.2× multiple a $9,600 add-back you cannot evidence is roughly $21,000 of purchase price you are about to lose in diligence. The recast worksheet in our report carries a documentation status on every line — supported, needs proof, likely struck — and totals two SDE figures: as claimed, and as defensible today. That is not a cleverness gap. It is a design choice about who the document is for.

4. It is not consistent, and consistency is what comparison requires

Ask twice, get two answers. Reorder the accounts, get a third. That is fine when exploring and expensive when you are comparing three acquisition targets, or explaining to a lender why the number moved between April and June. A fixed, printed method applied identically every time is worth more than a marginally better answer produced differently each run.

Questions people actually ask

Can ChatGPT value my business?

For a rough number, genuinely yes. Paste in a profit and loss statement and a competent model will identify the obvious add-backs, compute something close to seller's discretionary earnings, apply a plausible multiple, and explain what it did. It is fast, it is free, and it is roughly right. We would rather say that than pretend otherwise - anyone who has tried it already knows.

So what does a paid valuation report add?

A document rather than an answer. An itemized recast tied to source documents with a status on each add-back, a multiple tied to a named and dated transaction dataset and a specific size band with the derivation printed, a debt-service screen, a net-cash-to-seller waterfall, and a dated artifact with a stated basis and stated limitations that a franchisor's transfer desk, a credit analyst, or a buyer's accountant can read and check. A chat session is not that, however good the answer inside it was.

Where do your multiples come from, and where do a model's come from?

Ours are calibrated to published transaction data we name and date - size-band averages from IBBA / M&A Source Market Pulse (Q1 2026) and market context from BizBuySell's 2025 transaction data - and the report prints which band was used and why. A general-purpose model produces a multiple from its training data, without telling you the vintage, the source, or the deal-size mix behind it. The number may well be reasonable. You just cannot cite it, check it, or defend it.

Is an AI valuation consistent?

Not reliably. Ask the same model the same question twice, or change the order in which you paste the accounts, and the recast and the multiple can move. That is fine when you are exploring and a problem when you are comparing three acquisition targets or explaining to a lender why your number changed between April and June. A fixed method applied the same way every time is worth more than a slightly better answer produced differently each run.

Do you use AI in your reports?

Parsing a messy profit and loss statement and mapping it to a standard chart of accounts is exactly the kind of work software should do, and we use software to do it. The valuation method itself is fixed and printed in the report: the same size bands, the same adjustment factors, the same arithmetic, every time. The point is not that a machine is not involved. The point is that the method is written down and does not change between runs.
